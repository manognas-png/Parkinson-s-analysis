How the Parkinson's gait project works, step by step

Every step from the raw insole files to the classifier on the Pico: the maths, the code that does it, why each method was chosen, and how to read the numbers it produced.

0The whole project on one page

The question throughout: can the forces under the feet tell a person with Parkinson's disease (PD) apart from a healthy person of similar age, and which parts of the signal carry that information? Every step either builds features from the force signals, tests whether they separate the groups, or makes the result fit a cheap device.

1The data

PhysioNet Gait in Parkinson's Disease: 165 people, 93 with PD and 72 controls, from three studies with different protocols:

StudyProtocolPDControl
Ga (Yogev 2005)overground walking, plus a dual-task walk2918
Ju (Hausdorff 2007)overground walking (auditory-cue study)2925
Si (Frenkel-Toledo 2005)treadmill walking3529

Each person walked for about 2 minutes on insoles with 8 force sensors under each foot (L1–L8, R1–R8, in newtons), recorded 100 times per second. A file is a table: column 1 is time, columns 2–17 the 16 sensors, columns 18–19 the total force per foot. Sensors 1–3 sit under the heel, 4–5 the midfoot, 6–8 the forefoot. We used walk _01 (normal walking) for everyone, so each person counts once. The demographics file adds age, sex, height, weight, disease severity (Hoehn & Yahr, UPDRS), walking speed and the Timed Up-and-Go (TUG) time.

Loading is in src/load.py: load_trial reads one file into a pandas table, parse_filename turns GaPt13_01.txt into study Ga, group PD, subject GaPt13, walk 1, and load_demographics fixes the known typos (e.g. Juc010 → JuCo10, heights in cm vs m).

2Preprocessing: from a force signal to strides

2.1 Finding heel strikes

When a foot is in the air, its total force is exactly 0 N. A heel strike is the moment the force rises above 20 N after the foot has been in the air for at least 0.1 s (a real swing lasts about 0.35 s, so 0.1 s rejects flickers around the threshold).

src/gait.py

CONTACT_THRESHOLD_N = 20
MIN_SWING_S = 0.1

def detect_heel_strikes(force, time, threshold=20, min_swing_s=0.1):
    off_ground = force <= threshold
    # sample i is on the ground and sample i-1 was not
    rises = np.flatnonzero(off_ground[:-1] & ~off_ground[1:]) + 1
    n_swing = int(min_swing_s * 100)          # 10 samples
    # keep a rise only if the 10 samples before it were all in the air
    strikes = [i for i in rises if i >= n_swing and off_ground[i - n_swing:i].all()]
    return time[strikes]

2.2 Stride time, swing time and their variability

A stride is one full gait cycle of a foot: heel strike to the next heel strike of the same foot. Stride time = the difference between consecutive heel strikes (np.diff). Swing time is the part of the stride with the foot in the air: last toe-off before the next heel strike, to that heel strike.

Variability is measured with the coefficient of variation:

CV = 100 · standard deviation / mean  (%)A CV of 4% means stride times typically differ by 4% from the person's average.

PD makes walking less regular. Median swing CV was 3.8% in controls and 5.0% in PD.

2.3 Keeping only steady walking

A recording includes starting, stopping and turning, which make strides irregular in everyone. Those would inflate the CV and hide the PD effect, so we keep only steady strides:

  1. For each stride, compute the median of the 7 strides around it (rolling median).
  2. The stride is steady if it is within ±10% of that median.
  3. Steady strides that form a run of at least 10 make a segment; drop 2 strides at each end of the segment (the speeding-up / slowing-down edges).
def steady_stride_mask(strides, window=7, tolerance=0.10, min_run=10, trim=2):
    local_median = pd.Series(strides).rolling(window, center=True, min_periods=1).median()
    is_steady = (abs(strides - local_median) <= tolerance * local_median)
    mask = np.zeros(len(strides), bool)
    for start, end in runs_of_true(is_steady):
        if end - start >= min_run:
            mask[start + trim:end - trim] = True
    return mask

On the Pico this same rule runs in whole-number arithmetic (10·|2d − 2m| ≤ 2m, with d the stride length in samples and m the median) so the C code and Python agree exactly, even at the 10% boundary.

2.4 Resampling each stride to 100 points

Strides last different times (about 0.9–1.4 s). To compare their shapes, each stride is stretched or squeezed to exactly 100 points by linear interpolation: point k sits at k% of the gait cycle (0% = heel strike). The stride time is kept separately (as log duration) because resampling throws it away.

grid = np.linspace(start, end, 100, endpoint=False)     # 100 time points across the stride
resampled = [np.interp(grid, segment_time, channel) for channel in signals]
# result: (n_strides, n_sensors, 100), e.g. 6 sensors x 100 = 600 numbers per stride

2.5 Centering and scaling (for SVD and networks)

The 16 sensor signals are put in a matrix X with 16 rows (sensors) and T columns (samples). Two versions:

  • Centered: subtract each sensor's mean. Sensors keep their size in newtons, so big heel sensors weigh more.
  • Standardized: also divide by each sensor's standard deviation. Every sensor counts equally; this is the correlation view.

We reported both, because a result that holds in only one version is less convincing.

Body-weight normalisation (dividing forces by weight × 9.81) was used in the autoencoder and in one variant of the insole model. The firmware model uses raw newtons, because the device does not know the wearer's weight. The robustness step gave AUC 0.823 with body weight and 0.812 without; the intervals overlap, so the weight-free model gives up little.

3SVD: how many movement patterns does the foot use?

The maths

Any matrix can be written as a sum of simple "pattern × time course" pieces. The singular value decomposition is:

X = U Σ Vᵀ = σ₁ u₁ v₁ᵀ + σ₂ u₂ v₂ᵀ + … + σ₁₆ u₁₆ v₁₆ᵀ X: 16 sensors × T samples (centered). uk: a spatial pattern across the 16 sensors ("mode k"). vk: how strongly that pattern is present at each moment. σk: the size of mode k, with σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ … ≥ 0.

The modes are ordered so that mode 1 explains as much of the signal as one pattern can, mode 2 as much of what is left, and so on. The energy of the signal (sum of all squared values of X) splits exactly across modes:

energy fraction of mode k:  pk = σk² / (σ₁² + … + σ₁₆²)
effective number of modes:  Neff = 1 / (p₁² + p₂² + … + p₁₆²) Neff = 1 if one mode holds everything, 16 if all modes are equal. It is a smooth way to count "how many patterns matter".

How we computed it

Instead of decomposing the 16 × 12,000 matrix directly, we use the small 16 × 16 matrix X Xᵀ. Since X = UΣVᵀ, X Xᵀ = U Σ² Uᵀ: its eigenvectors are the modes uk and its eigenvalues are σk². This is fast and gives identical results.

src/features_math.py

def svd(X):
    eigenvalues, U = np.linalg.eigh(X @ X.T)          # 16 x 16, ascending
    order = np.argsort(eigenvalues)[::-1]
    U, s = U[:, order], np.sqrt(np.clip(eigenvalues[order], 0, None))
    Vt = (U.T @ X) / s[:, None]                       # time courses
    return U, s, Vt

def energy_fractions(s):  return s**2 / (s**2).sum()
def effective_n_modes(s): return 1 / np.sum(energy_fractions(s) ** 2)

In PD, one pattern dominates more and fewer patterns are used: the foot loads more "all at once", with less of the heel-to-toe roll that separates the sensors in time. Mode 1 is roughly "the whole foot pressing down"; later modes describe the heel-to-forefoot shift.

How to read it
  • P(PD higher) is the chance that a randomly chosen PD person has a higher value than a randomly chosen control (0.5 = no difference). 0.75 for mode-1 energy is a clear shift; 0.27 for effective modes means PD values are usually lower.
  • Look at both the centered and the standardized versions and at each study separately: the effect was strongest in Ga, present in Ju, weak in Si (treadmill).

4Sensor networks: which sensors move together?

Treat each sensor as a node and connect every pair with an edge weighted by how strongly their signals are correlated (absolute Pearson correlation |r|). That gives a 16 × 16 adjacency matrix A. Network maths then summarises it:

Laplacian:  L = D − A,  D = diag(row sums of A)
Fiedler value λ₂ = second-smallest eigenvalue of L λ₂ measures how hard it is to cut the network in two: large = tightly connected. Its eigenvector (the Fiedler vector) shows the natural split, which here was heel sensors vs forefoot sensors.

The feature we kept is heel–forefoot coupling: the mean |r| between heel sensors (1–3) and forefoot sensors (4–8) of the same foot. In a healthy heel-to-toe roll, the heel unloads as the forefoot loads, so they are only weakly related. Median coupling was 0.191 vs 0.211 in PD (P(PD higher) 0.64): PD feet load heel and forefoot more together, the same story as the SVD. It also survived the speed adjustment (section 6), which made it one of the most trustworthy features.

5DMD: the rhythm of walking

The idea

Dynamic Mode Decomposition looks for the best linear rule that moves the signal one step forward in time:

x(t + Δt) ≈ A · x(t) x(t): the sensor readings at time t. A: a matrix learned from the data. If this rule holds, everything about the motion is in the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of A.

Each eigenvalue λ of A is a complex number. Write λ = |λ| eiθ. Then the matching pattern (a DMD mode) oscillates with frequency θ / (2π Δt) Hz and grows (|λ| > 1) or decays (|λ| < 1) by |λ| each step. Walking is a rhythm, so we expect a dominant mode at the stride frequency (about 0.9 Hz) plus its harmonics (1.8, 2.7 Hz …).

The steps

  1. Window: the longest steady stretch of the walk, at most 10 s, centered.
  2. Downsample 100 → 50 Hz with a low-pass filter (scipy.signal.decimate) so Δt = 0.02 s.
  3. Delay embedding: 16 sensors are not enough "state" to predict the next step of a rhythm, so each column holds the last 50 samples (1 s, about one stride) stacked: a 800 × T Hankel matrix H.
  4. Exact DMD, rank 20: split H into X₁ (all columns but the last) and X₂ (all but the first), so X₂ ≈ A X₁. Instead of the huge A, compute its 20 × 20 version in the space of the top 20 SVD modes of X₁.
X₁ ≈ Ur Σr Vrᵀ (top r = 20 modes)
Ã = Urᵀ X₂ Vr Σr⁻¹  →  eigenvalues λ, eigenvectors W
DMD modes Φ = X₂ Vr Σr⁻¹ W,  frequency = Im(ln λ) / (2π Δt),  growth rate = Re(ln λ) / Δt

src/features_math.py

def dmd(H, rank, dt):
    X1, X2 = H[:, :-1], H[:, 1:]
    U, s, Vt = svd(X1)
    U, s, V = U[:, :rank], s[:rank], Vt[:rank].T
    A_tilde = U.T @ X2 @ V / s                  # 20 x 20
    eigenvalues, W = np.linalg.eig(A_tilde)
    modes = X2 @ V / s @ W
    omega = np.log(eigenvalues) / dt
    return {"frequencies": omega.imag / (2 * np.pi), "growth_rates": omega.real, ...}

Features and findings

  • Dominant frequency: the oscillating mode with the largest amplitude. It matched each person's stride frequency (median 0.94 Hz controls, 0.91 Hz PD).
  • Reconstruction error: rebuild the signal from the 20 modes and compare: ‖x − x̂‖ / ‖x‖. Median 0.27 vs 0.31: PD walking is a little less well described by a clean repeating rhythm.
  • Some eigenvalues came out slightly above |λ| = 1 (spurious growth). For reconstruction we put them back on the unit circle (stabilize=True) so the rebuilt signal does not blow up.
How to read it
  • Dominant frequency on its own did not differ much (P(PD higher) 0.44). After removing the effect of speed it did (0.67, p < 0.001): at a given speed, people with PD take more, shorter strides. That is a known PD sign, found here only once speed was accounted for.

6The speed problem and how we adjusted for it

People with PD walk slower (median 1.25 vs 1.08 m/s), and nearly every gait feature changes with speed. So a feature that "separates PD" might just be measuring speed. To check, for every feature:

  1. Fit feature ≈ a + b·speed + study effect using controls only (what is normal for that speed and study).
  2. Subtract that prediction from everyone: the residual says how unusual the feature is for that person's speed.
  3. Compare PD vs control residuals (Mann–Whitney test).

We used Huber regression rather than ordinary least squares: it uses squared error for small residuals but absolute error for large ones, so one odd control cannot drag the line.

huber = HuberRegressor(alpha=0, max_iter=1000).fit(X_controls, feature_controls)
residuals = feature_all - huber.predict(X_all)

7Evaluating fairly: subject-wise cross-validation

Why ordinary train/test splits are wrong here

If strides of the same person end up in both the training and test set, the model can recognise the person instead of the disease, and the score looks far better than it is (leakage). So every split is made by subject: all of a person's data is either in training or in testing.

Repeated stratified group k-fold

  • 5 folds: split the 165 people into 5 groups of about 33; train on 4, test on the fifth; rotate so everyone is tested once.
  • Stratified: each fold keeps roughly the same PD/control ratio as the whole dataset.
  • Repeated 10 times with different shuffles (seeds 0–9): one 5-fold split depends on luck of who lands together; 10 repeats average that out. That gives 50 train/test runs per model.

src/evaluate.py

for repeat in range(10):
    splitter = StratifiedGroupKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=seed + repeat)
    for fold, (train, test) in enumerate(splitter.split(X, y, groups=subject_ids)):
        model = clone(model_template).fit(X.iloc[train], y[train])
        scores = model.predict_proba(X.iloc[test])[:, 1]      # P(PD) for unseen people

Using the same seeds for every model means every model sees exactly the same splits, so differences between models are paired comparisons.

Nested tuning

Models have settings (hyperparameters) such as the regularisation strength C. Choosing C by looking at the test fold would leak. So inside each training set we run a second, inner 5-fold subject-wise CV (GridSearchCV with groups) to pick C, and only then score the outer test fold. This is nested cross-validation.

inner_cv = StratifiedGroupKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=seed)
logistic = Pipeline([("impute", SimpleImputer(strategy="median")),
                     ("scale", StandardScaler()),
                     ("model", LogisticRegression(class_weight="balanced", max_iter=5000))])
search = GridSearchCV(logistic, {"model__C": np.logspace(-3, 2, 11)}, scoring="roc_auc", cv=inner_cv)
search.fit(X_train, y_train, groups=subjects_train)       # groups keep the inner splits subject-wise

The pipeline matters: the imputer (fills missing values with the median) and the scaler (mean 0, SD 1) are fitted on training data only, inside every split.

8Classical machine learning (Step 7)

The two models

Logistic regression
Adds the features with weights and squashes the sum to a probability: P(PD) = 1 / (1 + e−(b + w₁x₁ + … + wkxk)). The weights are fitted by maximising the likelihood, with an L2 penalty (strength 1/C) that keeps them small and stable. class_weight="balanced" stops the model from favouring the larger PD group.
Random forest
300 decision trees, each grown on a bootstrap sample of people and a random subset of features; the forest averages their votes. Can capture non-linear rules and interactions. We tuned tree depth (2, 3, 5, unlimited).

Four feature sets

  1. speed only
  2. speed + age + sex
  3. gait: mode-1 energy, effective modes, DMD dominant frequency, heel–forefoot coupling, swing CV, DMD reconstruction error
  4. gait + speed + age + sex

Why we kept logistic regression

Reasons
  • It was as good or better. The forest never beat logistic regression by more than 0.003, and lost by 0.03 on speed alone.
  • Small data. With 165 people, flexible models overfit: a forest can memorise quirks of the training subjects. A linear model with a penalty has few parameters (one weight per feature).
  • Interpretable. Each weight says which direction a feature pushes the prediction.
  • Tiny to deploy. 6 weights and an intercept run on any microcontroller; a forest of 300 trees does not.

Permutation importance: what the model uses

Take the trained model, shuffle one feature's values among the test people (breaking its link to the label), and see how much the test AUC drops. A big drop means the model relies on that feature.

9Leave-one-study-out: does it work in a new lab?

Cross-validation mixes the three studies in every fold. But the studies differ (treadmill vs overground, different equipment and patient groups), so a model could partly learn "which study" instead of "which disease". The harder test: train on two studies and test on the third, for each of the three. This is leave-one-study-out (a leave-one-group-out split, with the study as the group).

src/evaluate.py

def leave_one_group_out(model, X, y, groups, test_groups):
    for held_out in np.unique(test_groups):            # "Ga", "Ju", "Si"
        test  = np.flatnonzero(test_groups == held_out)
        train = np.flatnonzero(test_groups != held_out)
        scores, _ = _fit_and_predict(model, X, y, groups, train, test, 0, 0)

10Feature and sensor selection: from 16 sensors to 6

A cheap insole should have few sensors. Two ways to choose them, which answer different questions.

10.1 Unsupervised: QR pivoting (which sensors best reconstruct the others?)

Take the top r SVD modes Ur (16 × r). If we measure only sensors S, we can estimate the mode amplitudes by least squares from those rows and then rebuild all 16 sensors:

a = (Ur[S])⁺ x[S],   x̂ = Ur a ⁺ is the pseudo-inverse. The rebuild is good when the rows Ur[S] are well-conditioned (not nearly redundant).

QR decomposition with column pivoting on Urᵀ picks sensors greedily: first the sensor with the largest mode loadings, then the one that adds most of what the chosen sensors do not already explain, and so on. It is the standard method in sparse sensor placement.

from scipy.linalg import qr
_, _, pivots = qr(modes.T, pivoting=True, mode="economic")
sensors = pivots[:n_sensors]                  # e.g. L5, L4, R8, L8, L3, R2 for 6

10.2 Supervised: greedy forward selection by AUC

Start from the best pair of sensors (scored by inner-CV AUC of the 3 gait features computed from that subset), then add whichever sensor raises the AUC most, up to 8. Crucially, this whole search runs inside each outer training fold (nested), so the chosen sensors never see the test people. A selection made on all data and then cross-validated would be optimistic.

Why we chose the anatomical six
  • QR is the wrong objective. It picks sensors that rebuild the signal well (its reconstruction error was the lowest), but the PD signal lives in the heel-to-forefoot relationship, which those picks miss. Good reconstruction ≠ good classification.
  • Anatomical matches the supervised search (confidence intervals overlap), while being one fixed set rather than a different set in every fold.
  • Explainable and buildable: one sensor under the heel, the midfoot and the forefoot of each foot, which is how commercial pressure insoles are laid out.
  • It met the success criterion stated before the run: mean AUC at least 90% of the 16-sensor AUC, and higher than the median of 100 random 6-sensor sets. Its ratio to all 16 sensors was 1.05 (CI 0.95–1.15).
Caveats
  • The QR sets were computed from the pooled data of all 72 controls, including controls that later sit in test folds. This can only flatter QR, which still lost, so the conclusion stands, but the QR AUCs are slightly optimistic.
  • The anatomical set was one of several candidates compared on the same cross-validation before it was chosen, so its 0.753, and the insole-only numbers built on it, may be slightly optimistic.

11Deep learning: what we tried and why it did not win

11.1 The convolutional autoencoder (Step 9)

An autoencoder is a neural network trained to copy its input through a narrow middle layer (the bottleneck). If it is trained only on healthy strides, it learns to rebuild healthy shapes well; a stride it rebuilds badly is "unlike healthy walking". The idea was an anomaly detector that needs no PD labels.

src/models.py

class GaitAutoencoder(nn.Module):              # input: 6 sensors x 100 points, body-weight units
    def __init__(self, n_channels=6, hidden=16, latent=4):
        self.encoder = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Conv1d(6, 16, 5, stride=2, padding=2), nn.ReLU(),     # 100 -> 50 steps
            nn.Conv1d(16, 16, 5, stride=2, padding=2), nn.ReLU(),    # 50 -> 25
            nn.Conv1d(16, 4, 5, padding=2))                          # bottleneck: 4 x 25 = 100 numbers
        self.decoder = nn.Sequential(
            nn.ConvTranspose1d(4, 16, 4, stride=2, padding=1), nn.ReLU(),   # 25 -> 50
            nn.ConvTranspose1d(16, 16, 4, stride=2, padding=1), nn.ReLU(),  # 50 -> 100
            nn.Conv1d(16, 6, 5, padding=2))
  • Conv1d: slides a small filter (5 points wide) along the stride, so the same shape detector works anywhere in the gait cycle. Stride 2 halves the length.
  • Training: Adam optimiser, mean squared error between input and output, on training-fold control strides only; 15% of control subjects held out for early stopping (stop when that validation loss has not improved for 30 epochs, keep the best epoch).
  • Score: each person's median reconstruction error over their strides. Higher = less healthy-looking.
  • About 3,900 parameters, 170,000 multiply-adds per stride: small enough for a microcontroller.

We compared it with two simple baselines that use the same idea: distance to the mean control stride, and PCA reconstruction error (a linear autoencoder with 10 components).

Why it did not perform well

Reasons
  • The task does not ask about PD. An autoencoder is trained to rebuild strides, not to separate groups. "Unusual" can mean heavier, faster, a different shoe or a different study, not only PD. A supervised model is told what difference to look for.
  • Too little data for the extra flexibility. Each fold trains on about 50 control people. Strides number in thousands, but strides of one person are near-copies, so the real sample is the people. The network has no room to learn more than the simple mean shape does, and the mean-distance baseline already captures that.
  • The PD difference is mostly a simple shift (slower, flatter heel-to-toe loading), which a linear method describes as well as a non-linear one.
  • int8 collapsed (0.69 → 0.56) because the differences between people are small compared with the rounding step of 8-bit activations: 256 levels across the whole activation range wash out a reconstruction error that is itself a small difference. The linear insole model later kept full accuracy at int16 (65,536 levels), see section 12.

11.2 The CNN speed and TUG regressor (Step 10)

A small CNN read one stride (6 × 100) plus its log duration and predicted walking speed and log TUG. Compared with ridge regression on the same 601 inputs:

12The final classifier: insole-only model

Goal: a model that needs nothing but the 6 insole sensors, with no stopwatch, scale or clinic visit, and that fits on a microcontroller. It was built in two versions: forces divided by body weight (AUC 0.823, needs the weight) and raw forces (0.812, sensors only). The Pico runs the raw version.

12.1 Stage 1: per-stride estimates

Each steady stride gives a 601-number input: 600 resampled forces (6 sensors × 100 points) + log stride duration. Two ridge regressions map that to walking speed and log TUG:

ŷ = w · f + b,  fitted by minimising  Σ (y − w·f − b)² + α ‖w‖² The penalty α‖w‖² shrinks the 601 weights. With 601 inputs and about 130 people, an unpenalised fit would memorise noise. α is chosen from 0.1 … 10,000 by an inner GroupKFold (strides of one person stay together).
def ridge_search():
    return GridSearchCV(Pipeline([("scale", StandardScaler()), ("ridge", Ridge())]),
                        {"ridge__alpha": np.logspace(-1, 4, 6)},
                        cv=GroupKFold(n_splits=3), scoring="neg_mean_squared_error")

Every stride gets its own estimate; the person's value is the median over their steady strides (the median ignores the odd bad stride). TUG is log-transformed because it is skewed (a few people take 20+ s). TUG is used only as a proxy for mobility and fall risk; no falls were recorded in this dataset.

Template deviation: the average healthy stride (mean of training-control strides) is the template; a person's score is the median over strides of the mean squared difference from it.

12.2 Stage 2: three gait features from the six sensors

From the 6 × 6 covariance of the sensor signals over the steady strides: energy in mode 1 and effective number of modes (section 3, from the eigenvalues of the covariance), and heel–forefoot coupling (section 4, mean |r| of the pairs heel–midfoot and heel–forefoot of each foot).

12.3 Stage 3: logistic regression on 6 features

est. speed, est. log TUG, template deviation, mode-1 energy, effective modes, heel–forefoot coupling → nested logistic regression (section 7) → P(PD).

12.4 Cross-fitting: the step that keeps it honest

The classifier is trained on estimated speeds. If those estimates came from a ridge model that had seen the same people, they would be too accurate on training people and the classifier would learn to trust them too much. So inside each outer training fold, a second 5-fold split produces estimates for each training person from ridge models that did not see them (cross-fitting). Test people get estimates from a ridge model fitted on all training people.

src/run_insole_regression.py

def insole_estimates(X, F, stride_subject, targets, is_control, train, test, seed):
    inner = GroupKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=seed)
    for inner_train, inner_test in inner.split(train, groups=train):
        fit, score = train[inner_train], train[inner_test]
        est.loc[score, "est_speed"]    = ridge_fit_predict(F, stride_subject, speed, fit, score)
        est.loc[score, "est_log_tug"]  = ridge_fit_predict(F, stride_subject, log_tug, fit, score)
        est.loc[score, "template_dev"] = template_scores(X, stride_subject, controls_in(fit), score)
    est.loc[test, "est_speed"] = ridge_fit_predict(F, stride_subject, speed, train, test)   # etc.

12.7 Centre of pressure (Step 11): the strongest features, but they need 16 sensors

Following Sifat et al. 2026 (arXiv:2609.22956), each stance phase is turned into a trace of the anterior–posterior centre of pressure: the force-weighted average of the sensors' heel-to-toe positions, from 0 (heel) to 1 (toe). The trace is resampled to 0–100% of stance, and AP-COP10 takes its median over stances in 5 windows (5–15%, 25–35%, 45–55%, 65–75%, 85–95%), giving the position (mean of both feet) and the left–right asymmetry in each window: 10 features.

q(t) = Σi Fi(t) yi / Σi Fi(t) Fi: force on sensor i; yi: its heel-to-toe position, scaled from 0 (heel) to 1 (toe).

13How to read the results

AUC
Area under the ROC curve. The probability that the model gives a random PD person a higher score than a random control. 0.5 = chance, 0.7 = fair, 0.8 = good, 0.9 = excellent. It does not depend on a threshold.
Sensitivity
Of the people with PD, the fraction the model flags (at probability ≥ 0.5).
Specificity
Of the controls, the fraction the model correctly calls typical.
Balanced accuracy
(sensitivity + specificity) / 2. Unlike plain accuracy, it is not inflated by the larger PD group.
Mean ± SD over repeats
How much the AUC moves when people are reshuffled into different folds (e.g. 0.823 ± 0.015). This is split luck, not how uncertain the true AUC is.
95% CI (bootstrap)
Resample the people with replacement 2,000 times (PD and controls separately) and recompute the AUC; the middle 95% of those values. This is the uncertainty from having only these 165 people. With 165 people, intervals are about ±0.06, so two models less than about 0.05 apart are usually not distinguishable.
Paired difference
When two models use the same splits and same bootstrap samples, look at the CI of their difference; if it excludes 0, the difference is real.
P(PD higher)
The rank effect size from the Mann–Whitney test: the chance a random PD person has a higher value than a random control. Its p-value says whether the difference could be chance.
Permutation importance
AUC drop when a feature (or group) is shuffled on held-out people. Larger = the model relies on it more.
MAE, R², Spearman ρ
For regressions: MAE is the average error in the target's units (0.098 m/s); R² is the fraction of the spread between people explained (0.67); Spearman ρ is how well the estimates rank people (0.78).
Per study and leave-one-study-out
A model that works only when all three studies are mixed may be learning the study. Look for similar numbers per study, and for leave-one-study-out scores close to the CV scores.
Mean over repeats vs pooled
Mean over repeats: compute the AUC in each of the 10 repeats, then average. Pooled: average each person's score over the repeats first, then compute one AUC (the bootstrap uses this). Pooled is usually a little higher. Compare like with like.
In-sample AUC
A model scored on the people it was trained on (0.850 for the final model). Always optimistic; never report it as performance.

14Limits to state in the report

  • 165 people in three lab studies; the confidence intervals are wide (about ±0.06 in AUC).
  • Study and protocol are mixed with the label (treadmill in Si), so per-study and leave-one-study-out results matter as much as the pooled AUC.
  • Speed dominates. Much of what separates the groups is slower walking, which also happens with age, pain or other conditions.
  • TUG is a proxy; no falls were recorded.
  • Not a diagnostic tool. The model describes how Parkinson-like a walk looks in this dataset. A clinical tool would need new, prospectively collected data and comparison with other conditions.
