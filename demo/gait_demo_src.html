Gait insole demo: Parkinson’s disease vs healthy walking

Pressure sensors in a shoe insole show how a person puts weight on the foot. Here, a healthy person and a person with Parkinson’s disease of similar age and walking speed walk side by side. Watch how the person with Parkinson’s lands with less weight on the heel and shifts pressure forward earlier, and try the 6-sensor version to see that a much simpler insole still captures it.

Footprints: each circle is one sensor; stronger colour = more force. The dot shows the centre of pressure (where the weight is concentrated) while that foot is on the ground. Trace: left-foot heel force (green) and forefoot force (purple) over the last 4 seconds, in body weights on the same scale for both people (for a file without a weight, body weight is estimated from the insole); dashed lines mark detected heel strikes. Cards: the large number is the median of all left-foot strides so far, with the latest stride below it, next to the group medians of all healthy people and all people with Parkinson’s in the dataset.

All participants: how weight moves through the step

Heel load through the stance phase (0% = heel strike, 100% = foot leaves the ground), as a fraction of body weight. People with Parkinson’s carry less weight on the heel in early stance (shaded 5–15%).

Centre of pressure from heel (0) to toe (1). In Parkinson’s it moves forward earlier, a sign of flatter, less heel-first foot contact. Bands show ± 1 standard deviation across people.

About these numbers

The healthy template (used for “deviation from typical”) is the average stride of all healthy participants, including the ones shown, so it is slightly flattering for them. The demo clips are 10 seconds at 50 samples per second, so their values differ a little from whole-recording analyses. The cross-validated results in the project report are the reference values; this page demonstrates the analysis pipeline, which reproduces the project’s Python code to rounding precision.

  • The deviation score divides each stride by an insole-estimated body weight (the force while only one foot is on the ground), so its values differ from the deviation scores in the project’s autoencoder analysis (Step 9).
  • The example pairs were chosen to be typical of each group, but individuals vary and the groups overlap: many healthy people load the heel less than many people with Parkinson’s.
  • With 6 sensors, the centre of pressure is a coarse estimate (only three points per foot), so an individual’s value can differ from the group pattern.
  • Deviation values for 16 and 6 sensors are on different scales; compare them only within one version.
Data: PhysioNet “Gait in Parkinson’s Disease” (Goldberger et al., Circulation 2000; Yogev et al. 2005; Hausdorff et al. 2007; Frenkel-Toledo et al. 2005). Centre-of-pressure features after Sifat et al. 2026 (arXiv:2609.22956).
